FREE SAMPLE / EVIDENCE LITERACY

A finding is not
the whole story.

Read a result. Find its boundaries.

A short reading lesson and practice exercise. No account, email or payment required. This is not the complete paid course or a certificate assessment.

Begin with five questions

Before accepting a headline, identify who or what was studied, what was tested, what it was compared with, what was measured, and how long the study lasted. Those details define the question the research can answer.

An observation in cells or animals can motivate further study. It does not establish that a treatment benefits people. A human study also needs careful interpretation: an observational association differs from a randomized comparison, and a trial can still have important limitations.

Study type is a starting point, not a substitute for reading methods and results.

FICTIONAL TEACHING EXAMPLE

Read the claim carefully

A laboratory tests an experimental compound in 24 mice. After 14 days, a tissue marker differs between treated and untreated animals. A social post says: “This proves the compound helps people recover.”

The example is invented to practice interpretation. It is not a report about a real compound or trial.

What changed—and what didn’t?

The reported observation concerns a tissue marker in mice. The headline changes the population to people and the outcome to recovery. Neither change is supported by the example.

A useful summary keeps the original boundaries: “In this fictional mouse experiment, the treated group had a different tissue marker after 14 days. The scenario does not establish a recovery benefit in people.”

It would also matter how the animals were assigned, whether outcome assessment was blinded, how the marker was analyzed, and whether any animals were excluded.

Make uncertainty visible

Ask what additional information is needed rather than replacing an unsupported positive claim with an equally unsupported negative one. This example does not prove a human benefit, but it also does not settle every future research question.

A practical habit: write two sentences—one describing the observation, the other explaining the limit. Keep both when you share the result.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Four questions. No pressure.

Your answers stay on this page and are not saved or sent. This practice does not count toward a certificate.

1. What population was studied?

The scenario describes mice only. Human recovery cannot be inferred from that population.

2. What was measured?

A tissue marker is the stated endpoint. It is not the same as a clinical recovery outcome.

3. Which conclusion stays within the evidence?

The cautious conclusion preserves both the observation and its limits.

4. What would help you appraise the experiment?

Study methods help interpret the result. Popularity and confidence cannot replace those details.
